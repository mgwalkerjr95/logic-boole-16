Logic Boole 16 quick start

Revision F · from the box to a first capture

What you need

In the box

  • The board. Nothing else: no cable, no leads, no spacers.

You bring

  • A computer: a Mac, or Linux (Debian, Ubuntu, Raspberry Pi OS). Windows is not supported.
  • A USB-C cable made for USB 3 (5 Gbit/s; often marked SS or 5 Gbps), to a USB 3 port of the computer: USB-C to USB-C, or USB-C to a blue USB-A socket. This one cable carries power and data. A USB 2 cable or port also works, for slow signals: the board then carries about a ninth as much.
  • Leads for your signals: jumper wires with sockets for 2.54 mm pins, or a 16-way (2 × 8) ribbon cable with an IDC socket for each pod.
  • Python 3.9 or later on the computer. Step 1 installs the rest; there is no driver.
  • For a Raspberry Pi only: four M2.5 × 16 mm spacers and eight M2.5 × 6 mm screws.
1

Get the computer ready (once)

Mac. With Homebrew:

brew install pipx libusb
pipx ensurepath

Linux, Raspberry Pi OS. libusb is already there:

sudo apt install pipx
pipx ensurepath

Then open a new terminal window and install the software: pipx install logicboole. On Linux, the first logicboole check prints a line of commands that lets your user open the board: run it once.

2

Plug the board in

Connect the board's USB-C socket to a USB 3 port of the computer. Leave the probes unconnected for now.

Mac laptops ask "Allow accessory to connect?" for a USB device they have not seen: choose Allow. macOS can ask once more in step 3, when the board comes back to USB under its own name.
3

Check it

logicboole check

This loads the board and captures a test pattern that the board makes itself. In a few seconds it prints:

Python 3.12.4: fine.
libusb: found.
The board: on USB.
The firmware: downloaded, checked and kept on this computer.
Loaded: firmware version 2, FPGA design version 5, serial number 0009021E1311241C.
USB: 5 Gbit/s.
A capture of the FPGA's test pattern, 100000000 samples at 200 MS/s: every word right.
A capture of the FPGA's test pattern, 2000000 samples at 10 MS/s: every word right.
The board is ready.  "logicboole" opens the app.

If something is missing, its line says what to do. The board keeps no firmware: the software loads it at every plug-in, and downloads it the first time (the computer needs the internet that once). Once loaded, the ARM light blinks once a second.

4

Connect your circuit

  • Ground first. Join any pin of a pod's lower row to your circuit's ground. Every pin of the lower rows is ground.
  • Then the signals, to pins of the upper rows: A0 to A7 on pod A, B0 to B7 on pod B. The names are printed on the board above the pods. Each input takes up to ±25 V.
  • Each pod has its own threshold, so pod A can watch 3.3 V logic while pod B watches 1.8 V.
  • For signals faster than a few megahertz, keep the leads short and put a ground lead beside the signal.
  • The trigger header is 3.3 V logic: − is ground, OUT goes high when a capture triggers, IN can be part of a trigger.
5

Take a capture in the app

logicboole

opens the app in your browser, at http://localhost:8016, with the board as its source.

  • Set the two thresholds to about half your logic voltage: 2.5 V for 5 V logic, 1.65 V for 3.3 V, 0.9 V for 1.8 V, 0.6 V for 1.2 V. They go from 0.3 V to 3 V.
  • Pick a rate and a length. Ten times your fastest signal or more is a good rate to start with; the fastest is 200 MS/s, a sample every 5 ns. Then press Start, or the space bar.
  • Auto-detect looks for I²C, SPI and serial in the capture and decodes them. Point at a pulse for its width and frequency.
  • To wait for an event, pick a channel and an edge under Trigger before you press Start.
  • No circuit at hand? logicboole --fake runs the app with a stand-in board that makes an I²C bus.
6

Go further

logicboole capture bus.sr --rate 10M --time 2 -a 1.65 -b 0.9 takes a capture from the command line into a file for PulseView. logicboole selftest is the long check of the board: 34 captures, about half a minute. software/README.md in the repository has every command, the triggers, the protocol decoders, the Python package, and letting an AI agent use the board.

If something does not work

What you seeWhat to do
"The board: not on USB."Try another cable and port. A cable made only for charging carries no data.
"macOS has not let it connect"Run it again and choose Allow when macOS asks. Setting: "Allow accessories to connect".
"this user may not open it"Linux: run the line it prints, then unplug the board and plug it in again.
"USB: 480 Mbit/s"The cable or the port is USB 2. The board works; fast signals need a USB 3 cable and port.
"Another program is capturing"Wait for it, or end it: logicboole load takes the board back.
A capture ends early: "overflow"Too many changes for the link: lower the rate, shorten the capture, or use USB 3.
An input stays at 0 or at 1Check the ground lead and that pod's threshold.
Anything else oddUnplug the board and plug it in again: it is then as it was.

On a Raspberry Pi 4 or 5

  • Set the board on the Pi's 40-pin header, on four M2.5 × 16 mm spacers held by eight M2.5 × 6 mm screws (yours to get: they are not in the box); it takes its power there. Join its USB-C socket to a blue USB 3 socket of the Pi with a short USB 3 cable, for the data. On a Pi 4 the cable's USB-C plug body must be no thicker than 6.4 mm.
  • Then steps 1 to 6 as on any Linux computer. With no screen on the Pi: logicboole --host 0.0.0.0, and open the address it prints on another computer, on a network you trust (the app has no password).
